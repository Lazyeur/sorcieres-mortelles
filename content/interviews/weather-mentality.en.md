titre: Weather Mentality: A Winter Before Spring
resume: A deleted folder, a demo from 2020 and an ADHD diagnosis: Margo tells us about alien funk, an EP made entirely at home.
---
*This interview was conducted in English: you are reading the original version.*

![Weather Mentality, with green hair and glasses, hugging a Telecaster guitar covered in stickers, surrounded by yellow hand-drawn stars](/img/weather-mentality-guitare.jpg "Weather Mentality. © Weather Mentality")

Q: Could you introduce yourself in a few words?

R: Hi! I’m Margo (my artist name is Weather Mentality) and I make indie music. I make all my songs myself at home, so I am a singer, producer and songwriter. Music is a way for me to process my emotions and express my inner world, so a lot of my songs are about my personal struggles and experiences, but I am happy other people relate to them as well.

Q: *alien funk* is your first project with several tracks, at least under this name, after a run of singles. Did you approach making it differently?

R: Yes, I had to change my approach. I could sometimes finish a song in a few days and sometimes it could take me up to 6 months, since I struggle with perfectionism. I was often waiting for inspiration instead of putting in the work consistently.

R: But I didn’t change my approach right away. I started working on an EP in autumn 2025, but at the beginning of spring 2026, I somehow accidentally deleted my whole music folder and my work from the past 8 months was gone. I was devastated since I hadn’t backed up my work, so I had to start from scratch. I didn’t want to spend months on just one song anymore, so I gave myself a deadline of 4 months to finish the whole thing. At first that seemed impossible, because I realized I didn’t like any of the songs I was initially going to include in the EP. The only song I kept was “mirrors”.

R: I stopped waiting for motivation to strike and decided to spend a few hours a day making songs after my 9-to-5 job whenever I could. I also had to limit the time I allowed myself to spend on lyrics, as this used to be the most time-consuming part of making songs for me. Now I would set a timer for 1 hour and try to finish the lyrics by the end of it. While that usually wasn’t enough, it still worked great for me: it now took a couple of 1-hour writing sessions rather than a few months just to finish the lyrics.

R: I changed the way I approach production too. Instead of “I will work on my music for 2 hours”, I made a list of things that didn’t sound quite right yet and focused on one thing at a time rather than the whole track at once. It was quite challenging, but having a more structured approach than usual helped me finish the project faster, so it took 5 months.

![Weather Mentality in a white T-shirt in front of a green hill and a blue sky, next to collages of a door open onto a meadow and an empty corridor](/img/weather-mentality-porte.jpg "© Weather Mentality")

R: Funnily enough, the track “alien funk”, which the EP is named after, wasn’t included until 4 months had passed and most of the work was completed. I was mixing one of the tracks when I realized its mood, style and genre were way too different to fit with the rest of the songs. I had to replace it, so I went through my old, unreleased demos and found an instrumental track from 2020 which became the foundation for “alien funk”. I used to feel guilty when I had a cool idea but couldn’t figure out how to turn it into a song. Turns out sometimes it just has to wait 6 years haha.

Q: What does your creative process look like? Do you start with the music or the lyrics? What tools do you use? Do you record at home?

R: I have several ways of approaching music, but all of them start with some sort of sound; lyrics always come after. Most of the time I make a whole song arrangement first, then play it on repeat, mumbling gibberish and building my lyrics off that. Some of the made-up words sound like real ones, so by association I come up with lines. Sometimes I use old lyrics from songs I don’t plan to finish or release, to see if anything could be used. I use my Notes app a lot as well, whenever I randomly get an idea, whether it’s a single word or a couple of lines.

R: Another way is a looper pedal and my guitar. It’s good for coming up with melodies, and if I really like one, I can turn it into a song later. This is my favorite way to unwind when I am overwhelmed and to let all my emotions out. It puts less pressure on me than opening my computer and thinking “alright, let’s make a song”, because all I can make is a loop, so I focus on expressing my feelings with sound and not on “which drum samples should I choose”.

R: If I already have some idea of what I want to write about, I come up with chords on guitar that fit the mood, complete the lyrics and record later. But I use this approach very rarely, as I often decide to switch things up in the arrangement, which can disrupt the rhythmic flow of the lyrics, so I have to rewrite certain parts.

R: When it comes to gear, I don’t have a lot of it, which is funny, because when I first started making music I thought I needed better tools to make better songs, and here I am now, 7 years later, with almost the same equipment but better songs. All the gear I use to make my songs is:

a Squier by Fender Telecaster electric guitar
a Steinberg UR22 mkII audio interface
a Shure SM57 microphone
a Fender Jaguar bass

R: I make my music in FL Studio on my almost 8-year-old laptop. For drums, I use different sample packs I have downloaded over the years.

![A black cat sitting on a desk, in front of a laptop and a screen both showing FL Studio](/img/weather-mentality-studio.jpg "The home studio. © Weather Mentality")

R: I record everything at home. It’s free, but it comes with caveats. For example, I have to use blankets, pillows and a camera stand to replace the acoustic treatment a proper studio has, so that when I record vocals they are clean, without room ambience.

R: It also turns finishing songs into trial and error, since I have to make sure they will sound fine on different headphones and devices. I can be sure a song is finished and everything sounds great, only to realize it sounds completely off on my other headphones, and have to go back to FL Studio and adjust. I often have up to 10 versions with “final” in the name until I’m happy with the result. Having a pair of studio monitors is great for this, but in a room with no acoustic treatment they can be inaccurate. Since I am renting an apartment, I cannot change much here. It can be annoying at times, but it’s also very convenient to be able to start recording as soon as I get an idea, without having to leave the house and pay for studio time.

Q: “customer service” is quite an unusual track. How did you come to write it?

R: I wrote “customer service” trying to process my feelings about getting an ADHD diagnosis. It felt like a curse and a blessing at the same time. A blessing because I now had an explanation for my struggles, a curse because it’s never going away. I have written similar songs in the past, not knowing why I am the way I am. One of them was called “kingdom of chaos”, which describes ADHD quite well. So in “customer service” I wanted to consolidate my struggles and express how it has always felt.

R: The way my brain works reminds me of calling customer service or some government institution and being on hold, listening to the same tune on repeat and having to wait. You’re not even sure anyone will pick up, but you also can’t do anything else at the same time, because someone could answer any minute. I struggle a lot with starting and finishing tasks; even something I like doing can be very overwhelming. As soon as I wake up, my brain feels like 3 different songs playing at the same time, with so many thoughts happening that silence can feel too loud: “I’m locked in a crowded room without an exit”. Another part, “I put my sanity for sale, my dreams were shiny, I wanted everything they have”, is about trying to fit in with societal expectations, trying to push through and giving 200% just to keep up with everyone else, which only leads to burnout and depression.

R: When I was a kid, I was constantly told “she’s talented but lazy” or “she should apply herself more”, while I was already working very hard but still making lots of mistakes because I couldn’t focus and got distracted easily. And that’s what the chorus is about: everything being total chaos, and feeling defeated despite giving it your best effort.

R: While “customer service” is full of frustration, with not much hope that anything could get better (“maybe the way will be clear one day, but for now it just slips away”), “alien funk” is a continuation of it and gives it a positive ending.

R: It’s also about being neurodivergent, but from a different angle: feeling alienated and not fitting in anywhere. At the end of the song, the main character finds other “aliens” who are just like them, and they don’t need to pretend to be something they’re not anymore. I had a lot of trouble with friendships throughout my life until my twenties. I don’t have many friends, but the ones I have love and accept me as I am, and they’re just like me, so they can relate to my experience.

Q: You also draw. Are drawing and music connected for you?

R: I’ve been an artist much longer than a musician: I started drawing as soon as I learned to hold a crayon, and I only picked up the guitar as a teenager. I didn’t write any original songs until 2018. For now, the only way those 2 passions are connected is that I make my own cover art for my music.

![Drawing of a lilac cat in an orange hoodie and green trousers, leaning on a skateboard, in a skatepark](/img/weather-mentality-chat-skate.jpg "Illustration by Weather Mentality. © Weather Mentality")

R: I’ve seen some people make melodies inspired by art, which is really cool, but for me it works the other way around: I prefer to make art inspired by music. That’s how I’ve done it for most of my life; I’d choose songs that fit the personality of the character I’m drawing or a feeling I am trying to express.

R: In the future I might use my art skills to make a music video or social media content for my songs, but for now the two are separate.

![Drawing of a calico cat in jeans and a green T-shirt doing a trick on a pink skateboard and making a peace sign, in front of palm trees](/img/weather-mentality-chat-ollie.jpg "Illustration by Weather Mentality. © Weather Mentality")

Q: In some of your songs, it feels like you blend love and pain, with very sensory words. Where does that mix come from?

R: For me, love and pain often go hand in hand. Love can be beautiful and tender, but it can also break your heart into pieces and leave a scar. And the other way around: pain can be excruciating, but it can also be freeing; it can show you why something you so desperately want isn’t good for you. Throughout my life I have experienced a lot of both, through friendships falling apart, breaking up with people I deemed “the one” and chasing my family’s approval.

R: I tend to give people many chances before I realize things won’t change. I used to think “all you need is love”, but unfortunately love means nothing when the other person is committed to misunderstanding you and protecting their ego instead of trying to solve a problem together as a team. That is what my song “the price of kindness” is about: “when you are starved for love you’ll lick it off of a knife”. Love can be painful, especially for people who were neglected in childhood, as they might stay in a bad relationship with the wrong person just because they want to be loved.

R: In my last relationship, my partner broke up with me 3 times, and every time they would change their mind afterwards and want to get back together. It was all quite turbulent, but I stayed because of how much I loved them, and my song “home in your eyes” is about this person. But love and patience have their limits. As painful as it is, the biggest act of self-love can be walking away.

R: But I don’t want to sound like I think all love leads to heartbreak. While I’ve had no luck in romantic relationships, I have amazing friends who make me feel loved. I have an unreleased song I made in 2020 called “best friend” (it’s only available on YouTube). It starts with “would you be my sun when the summer days are gone, would you be my air when my head is under water, would you be the arms to catch me when I’m falling down”. I’ve been through a lot in my life, but now I know my friend will be by my side whatever happens next.

Q: “mirrors” seems to deal with depersonalization. Is it something that touches you personally? Of course, feel free to share only as much as you want.

R: Sure, I have no issue talking about that. I wrote the lyrics for this song in 2023, but I didn’t like how the verses turned out, so it was never finished. When I was creating my EP *alien funk*, I remembered this track and decided to keep the chorus and outro and rewrite the rest. It was an interesting process, since I was writing about something I don’t experience anymore, at least not to the degree I used to, thanks to therapy. The song talks about anxiety, chronic stress and how they lead to dissociation.

![Weather Mentality, chin in hand, in front of shimmering water and big stitched blue stars](/img/weather-mentality-eau.jpg "© Weather Mentality")

R: My teenage years were quite traumatic, so even when I moved abroad to study, my old way of dealing with stress, by just disconnecting from my body and the world around me, didn’t go away. I was an overachiever and felt very strong pressure to succeed; my productivity measured my worth, hence the line “ran like a bat out of hell”, as a lot of my anxiety was caused by never slowing down and feeling guilty about resting. My life used to feel like driving a car with broken brakes at full speed: at some point a crash is guaranteed. I would sometimes look at myself in the mirror and feel like the reflection wasn’t mine. I knew it was, but it felt very alien. The world around me didn’t feel real (“the sceneries blending into a knell”); it’s like being in a dream, it feels “off”.

R: I wanted to express it in a rather abstract and exaggerated manner. For example, “still I can feel I’m not alone in here” isn’t about physically feeling someone’s presence or being paranoid; it’s about having so much anxiety, so many fears and worries, that they take up all the headspace and get in the way of getting anything done. “You live in every mirror hiding in plain sight, you all that I can hear I only see it now”: it’s easy to get stuck spiraling and ruminating, only seeing the negatives in everything, being self-critical and treating all of that as reality, while thoughts and feelings aren’t objective facts.

Q: What are the biggest challenges of making music as an independent artist?

R: For me, the biggest challenge is having to do everything myself while also having a full-time job. When you don’t have the money to hire someone to help bring your vision to life, or friends who are also musicians you could collaborate with, the only option is to do it yourself. And making a song requires learning a lot of different skills: playing instruments, singing, writing lyrics, composing, mixing, mastering, etc. I don’t mind that, as I am a curious person, I love trying and learning new things, and it’s nice to have control over what I make from start to finish, but it takes a lot of time.

R: It can be tricky to balance it with a job, chores, a social life, other hobbies and resting so as not to burn out. Some artists quit their jobs and succeed in music; I prefer to take it slow, since there’s nobody but myself who can support me financially, and there is no way to predict when my music will start earning me enough to pay my bills. So far, I have earned $50 from my music.

R: Despite that, I don’t think I would sign a record deal. There’s a lot of discourse online about how record labels often treat their artists badly. While there can be benefits to being signed, having a debt I must pay off and someone else owning the songs I made doesn’t sound like a good deal to me. Sure, I would love to be a full-time musician and be able to spend more time writing new songs, but just expressing myself through my music and finding people who relate to it is good enough for me.

Q: Is posting on social media something you enjoy, or more of a chore you have to do to get your art out there?

R: I used to absolutely despise having to post on social media, since I never had any interest in marketing or advertising. I thought like many other small artists: “Kurt Cobain wouldn’t be on TikTok, so why should I?”. But who knows? Maybe he would have been, and also, I am no Kurt Cobain. Just uploading a song and waiting to be discovered got me nowhere: I had 3 monthly listeners at most. I would keep making music even if nobody heard it, but I still wanted to share it with the world. I tried TikTok on and off from 2022, but after posting for a while and getting low views I’d usually give up.

R: Just about a year ago I took it seriously and started posting more frequently. Now I try to make at least one post every day. It is frustrating that artists sometimes have to post their song 600 times before it gets the recognition it deserves, and it’s especially demotivating when posts get only 100 to 300 views. But slow growth is still growth: I try to keep that in mind when posting, and not to measure the quality of my craft by the numbers it gets on social media.

R: While I would love to skip making TikToks and focus only on my creative process, I realize it’s the only way I have to reach people across the globe for free. So now I have a much more neutral attitude: it is still a chore, but I am thankful to have this opportunity.

Q: What emotion drives you to create the most?

R: I would say sadness. It can take many different forms, like feeling depressed, hurt, overwhelmed, lonely and more. Most of the songs I’ve released sound dreamy or even nostalgic rather than sad, even when they deal with struggles or topics that were once painful. That wasn’t always the case: I have many unreleased songs that sound rather dark, as I used to make post-punk, which is often associated with being cold and melancholic. I struggled with depression from the age of 13 and only started getting better at 23, when I could finally afford treatment, so a lot of my older music is full of raw pain and despair.

![Weather Mentality holding a black cat in their arms, in front of a green meadow and a deep blue sky](/img/weather-mentality-chat-noir.jpg "© Weather Mentality")

R: I still get depressed from time to time, but it’s not like it used to be. It’s more like a winter I need to get through, knowing that spring, with its warmth and blossoming, will eventually come. And that’s where the change in the instrumentals came from. I still carry a lot of sadness because of my past, but I don’t see it as something unbearable, rather as a natural part of being human. I’m hopeful and I trust myself to handle whatever happens next. It’s impossible to be happy all the time, because then it wouldn’t be anything special, it would just be the norm. Without sadness, the good and happy moments wouldn’t be so meaningful, I think.

Q: Is there an artist or a work you’d like to recommend to the readers of Sorcières Mortelles?

R: I absolutely recommend Magdalena Bay, especially their album *Imaginal Disc*. Their music is a big inspiration for me. I also adore the visual aesthetics of their music videos and cover art. It’s DIY, campy and whimsical, which makes it very charming.
