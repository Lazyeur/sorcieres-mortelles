---
titre: Neniu: Embracing Your Music and Growing With It
resume: From the Flower to Les Ailes, Neniu talks about songs and animated films that grow up along with him.
---
![Neniu sitting on a mushroom-shaped stool against a red background](/img/neniu-portrait.jpg "Neniu. © Neniu")

Q: Thank you for agreeing to this interview, especially for a project that is only just starting. I listen to your music a lot, and I discovered you during the promotion of *Arbres Infinis*, with the Flower. So my questions may be shaped by the way I understand your songs: don’t hesitate to tell me if I’m off the mark.

R: Yeah, I’ll tell you if that’s not it.

Q: I first discovered you through the Flower and its friends on social media. How did that idea come about? Was the album *Les Ailes* already in the works, or was it something older that you then reused for the promotion?

R: The Neniu project has existed since 2020, so that’s six years now. From the very beginning, it’s been a mix of songs and animated films, because I do both. My first album, *Moulin*, is an animated short film split into four episodes, available on YouTube, and then the audio album.

Between 2020 and today, the way people watch videos has changed, and that was true for me too: we went from rather long videos, in landscape format, to much faster formats. Basically, TikTok arrived. I’ve always been quite interested in the way humans consume images. I’m a bit of a content creator, so I’ve always been curious to see where people go to watch videos and how it all works.

After making a short film, I wanted to try formats closer to what you find on social media, TikTok or Instagram. I started experimenting and inventing new characters: some spoke directly to the person watching the video, others went on much faster adventures, over a minute. I tried lots of characters, and at some point the Flower was born. It was a character I had been drawing in my notebooks for a long time. I made it in 3D, started making videos with it, and it seemed to work a little. I thought it might be a good lead. And I liked that character; I felt there was a possible poetry with the Flower.

At first, the Flower wasn’t part of any album promotion at all. It was just a character I developed, that I loved and that worked. Then, little by little, it became part of *Arbres Infinis*. But originally, *Arbres Infinis* wasn’t supposed to be a song! In the video, the Flower met a snail and sang it a little song for its birthday. That video kind of blew up, even though it was just a twenty-second melody. It was my first TikTok hit.

When that happens, it’s quite overwhelming: one day I must have gained five or six thousand followers. I’m a very indie artist who does almost everything alone, so it stayed with me. I decided to continue that story. Everyone was asking me to turn it into a song, so little by little I wrote it. But *Arbres Infinis* really wasn’t meant to be one: it was a little melody in a video that had nothing promotional about it. After that, the Flower stayed on for my next releases, because I came up with other stories with it.

![The Flower, the snail and the disco-ball spider on a hill under a starry sky](/img/neniu-arbres-infinis.jpg "Cover of *Arbres Infinis*. © Neniu")

Q: That’s when I discovered you, during that boom.

R: That’s so funny, because a lot of people discovered me at that moment. Some people had been following me before, more for the short films, but a whole wave of new people arrived. It was a special time. I had already decided to make formats for social media, but that’s when I really realised I could produce videos fairly quickly and tell stories easily in that format. I started loving it and taking it more seriously. Since the Flower, in two years, I must have released more than a hundred videos, maybe a hundred and fifty. Spread out, that’s almost one video every four days: a pretty intense pace for animation.

Q: Yes, it normally takes a very long time to make.

R: It still takes a while: I spend about ten hours on each one, so it’s spread over two days. But I was all in.

Q: Speaking of which, is it the music that leads your 3D creations, or the other way round? For *Arbres Infinis*, you first created the little story with the characters, and the song came out of it.

R: *Arbres Infinis* is a special case: I composed something in a second for the video, and it later became a song. Normally it doesn’t really happen in that direction. The two are somewhat intertwined: I sometimes make tracks for the animation, but usually I write and compose the songs first. Once I have several, a universe takes shape, keywords stand out, and based on that I create my characters to go with the releases. So it’s mostly the music first. But sometimes it’s simultaneous, or the other way round: my characters go on adventures that give me ideas for music. It happens, but it’s rarer.

Q: In all the projects of yours I’ve seen, there’s a strong childlike aesthetic: the characters, the instruments, even the vocabulary of your lyrics. And it’s easy to link that to the theme of freedom, which comes back in several songs, especially *Moulin Avant* and *L’Air libre des gens qui rêvent*. Why is it so important to you?

R: The childlike side comes from the fact that it’s simple and colourful. When I create, I don’t tell myself “I’m going to make something childlike.” Rather, I tell myself I’m going to try to make something that as many people as possible can understand, in the simplest possible way. That goes for my videos as well as my songs: it isn’t convoluted, I make sure it’s easy to take in. I consider that I make pop, and in pop there’s always this idea of speaking to as many people as possible, of being universal. I have that in mind when I compose or make my videos, and it gives this rather simple touch.

What’s funny is that with simple words, simple images and simple characters, you can convey extremely complex and extremely specific ideas and emotions. They aren’t opposites.

There’s something else, too: simple words and simple shapes are how fairy tales and nursery rhymes are made. Animals stand for archetypes, go on adventures and pass on a moral. You find that in every story, even the most complex ones, like *The Lord of the Rings*. Going back to the essence of that is really interesting in short formats: the characters already carry their issues in their bodies. The Flower, for example, is rooted in the ground. Just by looking at it, you know lots of things about it: that it’s a fragile being, that it can’t move, that it may well die in winter. I don’t need to say it. These are figures everyone knows, and a flower playing guitar with its leaves is instantly evocative, and cheap.

That interests me a lot, because as humans, especially since the internet arrived, we’re steeped in images all the time. We have a very developed, dense and complex visual culture, and from there you can make something resonate in another human very quickly, just with an archetypal shape. The perfect example is smileys: a universal, super simple language that at the same time communicates a huge amount. With a colon and a bracket, you can tell someone you’re fine, or that you’re happy, with just two punctuation marks.

That’s what I’m looking for. The childlike side comes from searching for the simplest, most concise and most universal forms possible to express myself, and in the end it gives something a bit childlike. It’s also an aesthetic I embrace. Since I do a lot of things myself, I tend to do them in a slightly naive, almost ignorant way: I learned 3D on YouTube; as for drawing, I went to art school, the Beaux-Arts, but I didn’t really learn to draw there, so I’ve always drawn in a rather schematic way. At first it was somewhat by default, and it became an aesthetic.

Q: In the end, it’s the desire to create with the means you have.

R: That’s it, it’s a mix of all that.

Q: It may just be my impression, but on your latest album, *Les Ailes*, especially in the instrumentals, the childlike side of the older songs fades a little. The production is more complex, fuller, with sounds that feel less “childlike.” And the story of the Caterpillar that goes with the album, which turns into a butterfly, so which evolves and in a way becomes an adult, reinforces that impression. Is it intentional, to show a shift from “child” artist to adult artist? Or is it simply that your skills have grown and you make more complex things?

R: Indeed, it’s less childlike. The truly childlike moment was *Nid*, my very first EP. That one is super childlike, even in the lyrics, but I was about twenty. Then came *Moulin*, with somewhat darker subjects: the childlike side is still there, but it starts to fade. When I composed *Les Ailes*, I was between 27 and 28. I’m not a child at all anymore, nor a teenager: I’m an adult. The subjects I want to address and the way I compose change as a result.

![Drawing: a small character in a jumper reading “neniu”, with a purple bird in a nest on its head, under a big sun](/img/neniu-nid.jpg "Cover of *Nid*. © Neniu")

I don’t set myself many limits when I compose: I go where I enjoy it most, and usually that’s towards things I haven’t completely done yet. For *Les Ailes*, I really wanted to make my “little pop gem”; that’s what I called it while finishing it. Neniu’s full-on pop album, that was my goal. I’ve always made pop, but always a bit off the mark: lots of songs didn’t follow a verse-chorus structure at all, they were long, with really weird intros. This time I really wanted to go all the way with the pop format, because it interested me and because I felt I had that thing in me, held back by more arty desires. I come from a more SoundCloud, more indie scene, where pop isn’t always well regarded: there’s a kind of snobbery. *Les Ailes* was also about deconstructing that in myself and owning the fact that I’m an artist who makes pop. Putting myself on the cover with butterfly wings, a bit like a Christ figure, is very pop, very megalomaniac. I told myself: if I do it, I go all the way.

![Neniu with large black and red butterfly wings, floating in the sky inside a ring of flowers](/img/neniu-les-ailes.jpg "Cover of *Les Ailes*. © Neniu")

So there’s a change compared to before. The childlike side was starting to get in my way, because it wasn’t entirely me anymore. I want the Neniu project to evolve along with me, to be up to date. I don’t want to stay stuck in the image of who I used to be. So with each release there’s an evolution, and it’s always a bit stressful: you wonder whether the people who liked the previous project will like the next one. Nothing is certain. There are actually slightly different audiences for each project: people who loved *Nid*, others *Moulin*, others *Les Ailes*, others all three. I’m composing a new album right now, and it will be something else again, a new era, with new issues and new themes.

To sum up: it’s less childlike simply because I’m less of a child. The music grows up with me.

![The Caterpillar, green with big eyes, holding a small red computer in the tall grass](/img/neniu-chenille.jpg "The Caterpillar. © Neniu")

Q: You’ve already mentioned 3D, which you taught yourself on YouTube. What software do you use for your work?

R: For music, I use a cracked copy of Ableton. For video, at first I used Premiere Pro, After Effects and Animate, from the Adobe suite. Don’t get the Adobe suite: it costs money and it doesn’t work well! There’s free software out there. Now I only use Blender, which is open source, very well developed, with a huge community. Even my 2D animations, I make them in Blender. You can do everything with it, and it keeps getting better: the software’s rate of improvement is crazy. If you use Blender for a year, you see everything that has improved in that year.

Q: A question I like to ask people who create: which emotion pushes you to create the most? The one that makes you say “right, I need to put something out”?

R: At the very beginning, I think it was a feeling of loneliness, often linked to heartbreak, but sometimes simply to being home alone in the evening and feeling lonely. That happens, even without heartbreak. My very first songs were born in that context. Writing a song is like speaking. Whether it’s just sound or with your voice, it’s addressed to someone: you’re talking to someone, sometimes without knowing who. I feel you need a sense of loneliness to start with.

Today it has changed a lot. If you ask me which feelings are behind my writing now, there are plenty, a different one for each song. Sometimes it’s love, sometimes the urge to write about more political subjects. In the songs I’m writing at the moment, there’s one where I speak to boys in general, one where I speak to my eczema, one to a friend who is depressed… The people I’m addressing are very different, so the feelings are too. There are also songs where you talk to yourself to feel better, songs to reassure yourself, a kind of empowerment.

But I still feel you need a touch of loneliness. I’m not sure, other artists would probably say the opposite, but for me there’s always a bit of loneliness as a trigger.

Q: It’s true that loneliness makes you want to get out of it, and so to communicate something to others.

R: That could be it. It’s a bit like asking why humans make art. When you think about it, it’s still a bit absurd that humanity does all this. In the end, it’s a way of leaving a trace to reach out to another living being, to be understood, to not be alone.

Q: I have two or three silly questions that follow on from that one.

R: Go ahead.

Q: If you had the chance to forget how to count and learn it all over again, would you take it?

R: I often say I’m trying to unlearn how to count. To be clear: knowing how to count is handy, to know whether you’re buying a box of six or twelve eggs. But when you make music, you’re swimming in numbers: streams, likes, shares. For me, that’s harmful to creativity. They shouldn’t be indicators we take into account. It’s toxic for art, and even in life: we shouldn’t make our choices based on what works or not, but on what we deeply want. Yet we’re conditioned by those numbers.

When I say unlearn how to count, I mean unlearn how to count those numbers. From the moment I started putting my songs online, I ran into what works and what doesn’t, and that changes how you see what you do and the way you create. Sometimes I think it’s too late: I can no longer ignore whether something works or not. I’ve made my peace with no longer being free of all outside influence. When I release a song, I check whether it’s being streamed. But ideally, I’d love to be detached from it. To have the presence of mind to know it doesn’t matter… except it does in a way, because I work under France’s scheme for performing artists, the *intermittence*, and my status depends somewhat on what works. I wish I had the luxury, or the presence of mind, not to think about it.

So to answer your question: if I could unlearn how to count streams, I would, and I wouldn’t learn it again.

Q: You’re turning my silly questions into clever answers!

R: No, it’s a good question.

Q: Speaking of streams: to share your music and try to make a living from it, you more or less have to go through platforms like Spotify or Deezer. And we know that Spotify in particular does some not-so-glorious things with its money: artificial intelligence, funding wars… As an artist who is quite committed in your songs, how do you feel about being more or less forced to publish your music there?

R: First of all, you’re not forced to. You can have a musical life without being on streaming platforms. It’s a different path, maybe harder depending on what you do. If you make pop, it’s probably harder, but it’s possible.

For me, it’s a bit complicated in my head, because I haven’t really made up my mind yet; I ask myself the question every time. In practice, for now, I release everything on the platforms. For *Les Ailes*, I tried something: the album came out physically with fourteen tracks, and on streaming with eleven. The fourteen-track version arrived on the platforms six months later, which made a bonus for people listening on Bandcamp or on a physical copy. But I don’t think it went far enough. For the next one, I’d like four or five tracks to be only on the physical version and on Bandcamp, and never come out on the platforms. I’m going to talk to my label about it, but I think they’ll be up for it.

That said, I admit that streaming platforms account for a very large share of the people who listen to me. Without them, it would be much harder. It doesn’t bring in a huge amount of money, but a little all the same. I couldn’t exist completely outside of them: either I don’t have the courage, or I know it wouldn’t work, I don’t know. For now, I can’t see myself doing completely without them. On the other hand, I’m going to make more room for more ethical ways of sharing my work.

It’s the same question with social media: Instagram, X and Facebook are all run by people who are, let’s face it, huge neo-Nazis. Ideally, we should develop other, more ethical networks. There have been moves towards Mastodon or Bluesky. I’m on them, I put my stuff there when lots of people were migrating, but it didn’t really catch on. I keep an eye on what’s happening there, and I try to find in-between solutions.

It’s a complicated question, because my whole project depends heavily on the internet, and the internet has been massively privatised over the last ten years, for the benefit of truly horrible people. It’s as if my guitar, the instruments I use every day, belonged to horrible people, and every time I played I gave them money. You wonder what to do. The question isn’t settled for me: I’m just trying to find small alternatives.

Q: I completely understand, it’s not an easy situation.

Q: Second silly question: a week ago I listened to your project with Jouvence. Are you more “nanani” or “nananère”?

R: You can’t have one without the other, it’s yin and yang! But if I had to choose, I’d say “nanani,” because it can be used in two ways: in “nanani nananère,” to tease someone, like “nyah nyah,” and in “et nanani,” the way you’d say “et cetera.” So I’m more “nanani.”

Q: You mentioned it earlier: what’s your favourite nursery rhyme?

R: I love that you’re asking that, it’s funny.

Q: I’m trying to ask the right questions!

R: Good. I really like the song about the stake: “And if we pull, it will fall…” Do you know it?

Q: Never heard it. What’s it called?

R: Darn, I’ve forgotten the name. It comes from the south, maybe from Spain, and in French it’s called *L’Estaca*, or “the stake.” It’s a revolutionary song, but a gentle one, and so beautiful. It tells the story of an old man who, all his life, pulled on a stake to bring it down. The stake stands for imperialism, a ruler, a wall. The old man knows he will soon die, and he tells someone younger to take over: “Pull on this stake, and one day it will fall.”

It’s quite beautiful, a kind of activist self-sacrifice. You may spend your whole life as an activist, and the victories will be deferred: you may not live to see them. But you’ll have put your time and energy into more freedom for the next generations, or at least paved a way, set an example for those who come after, even if you don’t see the end of the dictatorship, or of capitalism, in your lifetime. It’s deep and powerful, and at the same time very simple.

Q: I’ll listen to it.

R: It’s beautiful. Funnily enough, I saw on TikTok that it’s being sung again at protests. I had the impression nobody really knew it, but actually they do, and in any case it’s having a revival.

Q: In *Amoureux à vie* and *Arbres Infinis*, there seems to be a certain fascination with old age and the wisdom that comes with it, almost in opposition to the childishness present in the rest of your art. For you, are these two things incompatible, or do they complement each other?

R: No, I don’t think it’s in opposition to childhood.

Q: Maybe I’m completely off the mark!

R: No, I’m thinking about what’s in those songs. Actually, I’m not that fascinated by the wisdom you gain as you get older. On the other hand, something does happen as you grow up. I like learning things. As you grow up, you don’t necessarily understand, but you understand more things, and that makes you ask even more questions. But it isn’t in opposition to some supposed childishness of children.

In *Arbres Infinis*, that tree is mostly a kind of god, a god of nature. There’s wisdom, but not necessarily linked to its age: we don’t know when nature was born. It’s more the idea of this thing we are part of.

Q: In quite a few of your songs, especially on the latest album, there’s a lot of empathy. I feel you talk a lot about others, about what they feel and how it can affect you. Do you think that, in the current situation, which has been going on for a few years in France and more generally in the world, we could solve a lot of problems if we all showed a little more empathy?

R: It might be a bit simplistic to put it that way. If we’re talking about solving the world’s problems, the rise of fascism, the genocide in Palestine, the situation in Sudan, I think it’s above all a question of how wealth is distributed. Does the distribution of wealth have to do with empathy? To some extent, yes: you need empathy to be able to share. But I think what we need most of all is a worldwide revolution.

Saying “if we were all nice, everything would be fine” would be a bit starry-eyed. I’m speaking from a privileged position: I’m in France, I’m white, I’m one of the privileged of the Earth. I couldn’t allow myself to say that. More empathy would always be welcome, but it would be naive to think it would fix everything. Honestly, I’m counting more on a general awakening, a kind of worldwide, permanent revolution, for more equality and for the world to be better. And we’re sort of in it: some quite beautiful things are happening right now for activists. It’s a hopeful moment.

![The Flower on a flowery hill, next to an Antifascist Action flag](/img/neniu-fleur-drapeau.jpg "The Flower. © Neniu")

Q: That’s true.

Q: A more classic question: when people listen to your songs, and more broadly your art, since you don’t only make music, if there’s one thing you hope they take away, what would it be? An idea, a feeling, anything.

R: I often try to keep my music from wallowing in sadness. There is sadness, I don’t want to pretend there’s nothing sad. But I’d like people to take away something reassuring and, overall, hopeful. That’s why I make music: to reassure, to ask questions and to bring hope.

Q: Hope is beautiful.

Q: One last little question: will we see the butterfly again in future projects?

R: It has well and truly flown away! I don’t even know where it is, it’s gone off into the sky, I don’t know what it’s up to. It comes back sometimes: it’s there at every concert. But in videos, I don’t think so. Maybe as a cameo, but I don’t think so. Now it’s living its own life, it’s independent. Maybe it’ll drop by from time to time. You know what teenagers are like: they leave home, they take the car…

Q: That’s great. I don’t have any more questions, unless there’s a particular subject you’d like to talk about.

R: No, it was great, perfect.

Q: Thank you so much for your time.
